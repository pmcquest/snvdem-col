Validation with Existing Data: Comparison with Rojas-Betancur’s CdDS Index

McQuestion, Coppedge, Sisk, and McMann

2026-08-09

Our fourth validation test compares our estimates against the most comprehensive alternative measure of municipal-level democracy in Colombia currently available. We use the Calidad de la Democracia Subnacional (CdDS) index, developed by Milton Rojas-Betancur at the Universidad de Medellín’s Observatory on Subnational Democracy. Rojas-Betancur (2021) draws on Leonardo Morlino’s (2012) “Theory of Democratization” (or, TODEM) framework to construct an index of seven dimensions: rule of law, participation, electoral competition, electoral accountability, institutional accountability, civil liberties, and solidarism (education and health coverage). Rojas Betancur groups these into two factors–procedural and substantive–selects one to three indicators for each one, and standardizes each a 0-100 scale using quantitative data from Colombian official sources. His dataset covers all Colombian municipalities from 2016 to 2023, overlapping temporally with our own index over eight years and across roughly 975 municipalities per year.

The two indices share important design features. Both seek to capture more than narrow electoral competition, both draw on administrative and observational data from Colombian government and third-party sources, and both cover all or nearly all municipalities rather than a selected subset. At the same time, they differ substantially in conceptualization, measurement strategy, and weighting procedure. Our index uses a liberal democracy framework anchored in V-Dem, emphasizing free and fair elections and civil liberties protection as assessed by country experts; the CdDS adopts a “democratic quality” framework that also encompasses institutional accountability and social rights (solidarism), with no expert or subjective components. Crucially, our index derives its weights from expert judgments about which local characteristics predict democratic variation, while Rojas Betancur assigns equal weight to each dimension. These differences lead us to expect a positive but moderate correlation: the two indices should share a common underlying signal about democratic conditions across Colombian municipalities, but they are not measuring the same thing.

The results confirm this expectation. Table 4 reports Pearson and Spearman correlations between our overall sndem index and the CdDS composite score for each year from 2016 to 2023.

Correlations are consistently positive and statistically significant, ranging from 0.22 to 0.44 (Pearson) and 0.25 to 0.46 (Spearman). The indices agree most strongly in the early years of overlap (2016–2018) and show a notable dip in 2019–2021, recovering thereafter. This pattern is plausible: the dip may correspond to the period immediately following the 2016 FARC-EP demobilization, during which our index–which incorporates conflict and civil unrest variables weighted by expert judgment–may have responded differently to the uneven security improvements than did Rojas-Betancur’s purely quantitative indicators.

Figure 9 presents the cross-correlations between our component measures and each of Rojas-Betancur’s seven dimensions. The component-level correlations are revealing. Both our electoral fairness (snelect) and civil liberties (sncivlib) components correlate most strongly with institutional accountability (A_Inst, \(r \approx 0.49\)–\(0.51\)) and solidarism (Solid, \(r \approx 0.46\)–\(0.50\)). This is not surprising: institutional accountability in the CdDS is measured largely by DNP’s Integral Performance Index–the same fiscal performance measure we use as a geopredictor for economic development–and solidarism captures education and health coverage, which are correlated with the socioeconomic conditions our index uses as predictors of democratic quality. Both indices therefore pick up a shared socioeconomic signal. Correlations with Rojas-Betancur’s rule of law, participation, and electoral competition dimensions are near zero, reflecting genuine measurement differences: his participation measure is voter turnout and his competition measure is party alternation, neither of which enters our measurement approach directly.

The negative correlation between our civil liberties component and Rojas-Betancur’s civil liberties dimension (\(r \approx -0.28\) to \(-0.30\)) likely reflects a conceptual difference. His civil liberties dimension takes the average of total homicides and the volume of tutelas (judicial petitions invoking constitutional rights) successfully resolved per municipality per year. More tutelas filed in a jurisdiction can reflect either more violations of rights or greater civic legal literacy and institutional engagement; more homicides, however, presumably restrict civil liberties. This more “negative” interpretation civil liberties contrasts with our civil liberties measure, anchored in V-Dem expert assessments of civil liberties understood more broadly as the degree to which the state protects rights, independent of whether citizens know to petition for them. The negative sign therefore reflects a real conceptual difference rather than measurement error in either index.

Taken together, these results support the validity of our method. The moderate positive correlations between the two overall indices — despite their different conceptual frameworks, weighting schemes, and data sources — indicate that both capture meaningful and partially overlapping variation in democratic conditions across Colombian municipalities. The component-level patterns clarify where the two approaches converge (on institutional performance and socioeconomic conditions) and where they diverge (on electoral processes and civil liberties operationalization). Rather than contradicting our measure, these divergences illuminate what is distinctive about each approach, and they underscore the value of the thick, expert-weighted, and multi-dimensional conceptualization our method provides.
